Working papers

  1. The Impact of Daylight Saving Time on Test Scores in Brazil

    with Naercio Menezes Filho, Bruno Kawaoka Komatsu and Tuffy Licciardi Issa
    Abstract

    Daylight saving time (DST) has been implemented in many countries to reduce electricity costs. In this paper, we explore the discontinuity of the mandatory DST across Brazilian municipalities to estimate its impact on average test scores of a large-scale high-stakes national exam between 2013 and 2019. Since the DST was extinguished in 2019, we use a geographic difference-in-discontinuities framework, which is robust to compound treatment and time-invariant sorting across municipalities. We find a positive impact of the end of DST on the change in performance of affected municipalities. The number of days between the introduction of DST and the start of the exams does not seem to make a difference for the estimated effects.

  2. Payroll Tax Relief and Labour Market Informality: Evidence from Brazil

    MSc dissertation, London School of Economics
    Abstract

    This paper estimates the effect of Brazil’s 2011 payroll tax relief program, the desoneração da folha, on labour market informality at the sector level. Using microdata from the Pesquisa Nacional por Amostra de Domicílios Contínua (PNADc) and exploiting the staggered rollout of the reform across sectors, I apply the imputation estimator of Borusyak et al. (2024) to a panel of sector-quarter observations spanning 2012 to 2014, benchmarked against three alternative estimators. The estimates point to a positive but statistically insignificant average treatment effect on the informality share, consistent across all four specifications considered. This null result aligns with existing evidence suggesting that informality is not a primary margin of adjustment to payroll tax changes in Brazil. Beneath the aggregate null, cohort-specific estimates reveal meaningful heterogeneity: the two treated cohorts point in opposite directions, with the overall null reflecting their weighted average rather than a uniform zero effect. The dynamic pattern warrants further investigation, but its causal interpretation remains uncertain given the data limitations inherent to household survey data.
